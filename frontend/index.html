USD/ZAR exchange rate

Background

Oil and the Strait of Hormuz

Extra context for the numbers above. None of it goes into the prediction. About a fifth of the world's oil normally passes through the Strait of Hormuz (EIA). Disruption there since the conflict with Iran began at the end of February 2026 has pushed oil prices up and made oil that can be shipped right away scarce.

Brent crude: futures vs. physical cargoes

The Brent price in the news is the futures price, for oil delivered in about two months. Dated Brent is the price of physical cargoes loading in the next few weeks. Usually the two are within a dollar or two of each other, but when supply is short, oil you can get now costs more. CEF's figures follow spot prices for physical petrol and diesel, so a wide gap means real import costs are higher than futures suggest. The estimates above are built from futures prices, re-fitted every hour to CEF's real figures.

Sources: ICE Brent futures via Yahoo Finance and U.S. Energy Information Administration, Europe Brent spot price.

Strait of Hormuz: ships visible on tracking (AIS)

Cargo ships and tankers the IMF's PortWatch saw crossing each day from their ship-tracking (AIS) signals. Ships with tracking switched off aren't counted. Each bar is one day, the line is the 7-day average, and the dashed line is the normal daily level in the year before the conflict. Dotted lines mark the start of the conflict and the ceasefire from the Islamabad Memorandum (17 June 2026) until fighting resumed (8 July).

This shows ships crossing openly, not how much oil gets through. Many tankers now cross with their tracking switched off, and PortWatch only counts ships that broadcast. Kpler, which adds these “dark” crossings back, put oil through the strait at about 14 million barrels a day in the week to 18 October 2026, around 82% of the level before the conflict. Adding the Saudi and UAE pipelines that carry oil around the strait to the Red Sea and Fujairah, oil leaving the Gulf was above pre-war levels, at more than 24 million barrels a day (Kpler via the Wall Street Journal). A low count here means few ships are crossing in the open; it doesn't mean the oil has stopped. Other trackers of dark crossings: Lloyd's List, Windward.

Source: IMF PortWatch.

How this works, and what to trust

Every day already published by CEF (cefgroup.co.za) is read directly from their own daily report, so that part matches CEF exactly. The one or two most recent days CEF hasn't published yet are estimated from US petrol (RBOB), diesel (heating oil) and Brent crude futures read at 15:00 London (just before the London Platts assessment that CEF's figures are built from), plus the USD/ZAR rate. How much each market counts for each fuel is re-learned every hour from how CEF's own figures have tracked them over the past year. Tested against 16 months of CEF history, this is typically within about 1.3% of CEF's real figure. It is still a proxy, not the real Mediterranean Platts assessment, so it can miss on days when European prices move differently from US markets. The predicted price move is the average over/under recovery for the whole review period: the days so far, plus the rest of the period assuming the BFP stays where it is now, each measured against the price CEF will compare it with (early in a period, the first few days still use the previous month's price). The actual government announcement (effective the first Wednesday of the month) can differ because it also folds in separate slate-levy and fuel-levy decisions. The slate levy adjusts itself every month, usually by a few cents a litre. Fuel-levy changes are announced by the Minister of Finance, usually in the budget; once one is announced for the coming change, the forecast adds it and says so.
